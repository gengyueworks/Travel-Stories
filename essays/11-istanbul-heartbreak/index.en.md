# Istanbul: Your Heartbreak Will Become a Unique Landscape in the Past

*Geng Yue · Travel Stories*

![Istanbul](images/002.gif)

Dear you, who just opened this article:

I have been on the road for nearly a month.

In the background, messages like these have been coming in, one after another:

![Reader messages](images/003.png)

You know I read every single one.

Every night, I also receive a goodnight message from a stranger — for perhaps three hundred days now. I have almost never replied, but this person knows I will see it, and keeps sending, sharing the weather in their city, reminding me to dress warmly when it gets cold. That is a kind of understanding.

Even on days with no updates at all, new friends still find their way here every day, sharing their travel stories and the joy of discovering this space. I am grateful.

The other media channels I manage keep updating daily, noisy and routine. But the softest part of my heart, I would rather entrust to some quiet corner — at least for those who understand.

To everyone who follows this account: we have talked. We know which cities each other lives in. Some of you have received handwritten postcards from our volunteer team.

**When you ask me anxiously, again and again — why haven't you updated? Are you too busy? Did you forget? Are you okay? Are you sick?**

Every departure brings growth, surprising encounters, and also the confusion of pausing to ask: where to next? Your concern from afar, reaching me in this freezing northern winter of howling winds and visible breath, feels like the warmth of a cup of hot tea.

Whatever happens, my volunteer team and I should keep going — because **every bit of concern you send is trust.**

A senior once told me: **"The hardest thing between people is trust. And in you, I see one hundred percent trust. That is extraordinarily rare — especially at my age, having seen so many people, witnessed so much cruelty, struggle, and darkness in human nature."**

But I have encountered absolute purity and trust. Perhaps it is a matter of like attracting like. Today, I am sending this article to everyone who has been thinking of me. This is the least we can do: **a gesture of trust and reassurance.**

Happy winter. **I am well. And you?**

Yue

Perhaps my favorite is this: "To watch the cypresses, the forests in the valleys, the neglected empty villas and the rusted hulls of old ships, to watch the poetry composed by the ships and the yalis on the Bosphorus, to set aside the grudges of history and enjoy it all like a child, hoping to know more of this world, to understand more" — a fifty-year-old writer gradually coming to understand that this awkward struggle is called joy.

Istanbul and Lisbon are alike. Both were once glorious empires, now set under the setting sun. Their streets and alleys lay bare fragments of poverty; even the stray dogs share the same melancholic sleeping posture. But perhaps it is precisely this fate that nourishes their souls. What history gives a city is not merely ruins and rubble, but a twilight demeanor. **If Istanbul were stripped of what Pamuk calls huzun, perhaps it would not deserve the sea breeze of the Bosphorus.**

**The Twilight of Civilization**

The deep twilight of the Bosphorus — of Istanbul — existed long before religion and greed, long before caravans and crusaders divided East from West. Republics, Ottomans, and even Byzantine armies conquered this place. The greatest art and civilization flowed through here. Bulldozers, houses, and collective memory have collapsed. Between what is seen and what is true, there will always lie the distance of forgetting and death.

![Twilight of civilization](images/019.jpg)

![Hagia Sophia](images/020.jpg)

One of the most sorrowful scenes in European history unfolded here: in the final hours before the fall of Constantinople, the last requiem mass at Hagia Sophia. The last requiem mass of the Eastern Roman Empire. The Byzantines, besieged and doomed, gathered here to pray to God one last time. Two days later, the Christian altar was dismantled. The cross that had stood for a thousand years fell to the ground. The most magnificent church in the Western world became the Sultan's mosque.
